Mundipagg's Design System dark mode redesign

Helping the developers, delighting the users. Or vice-versa.

The challenge

In 2019 Mundipagg's (currently Pagar.me, of Stone.co) Design System had a problem: a small color palette was getting in the way of an effective dark mode and causing accessibility problems in the UI with low reading contrast.

Introduction

We created the dark mode option in the DS (called Emerald) as a proof of concept and example of themes and use of components. However, it ended up instantly preferred by the staff of developers working on the product's dashboard (devs love dark mode, am I right?).

We quickly noticed that our dark mode option was not optimal, and that kick-started a more in-depth investigation. We needed to make the color palette in our design system more adequate to complex colorways, such as a nice and comfortable dark mode for our developers. Moreover, a redesigned color palette would help us tackle accessibility problems in our UI caused by a limited color palette.

My role

Product Designer, UI Specialist leading redesign process: investigation, testing, defining, mapping and implementing the choices in color palette to be integrated in the design system.

The team

Carla Vieira, Gabriela Schirmer and Ronalson Vargas were the frontend developers responsible for the design system, called Emerald.

Timeline

Late 2019.

Reality

We made Emerald DS with:

  • 4 brand colors (primary, secondary, tertiary and link color) and respective shades and tints on light and dark mode;
  • 1 neutrals palette in light mode and 1 in dark mode (each with 6 different shades);
  • 4 semantic UI colors (success, error, warning and information) with respective shades and tints.

Goal

We identified in Emerald the need for some alterations regarding the current color palette and more importantly the expansion of the palette to comprise a more robust colorway to support different uses on the applications, specially on the dashboard.

To attain such goals, we suggested the following changes:

  • 1 neutrals palette in light mode and 1 in dark mode, optimized for each theme;
  • Reformulation of 4 semantic UI colors (success, error, warning, and information) with respective shades and tints - prioritizing high contrast score and dark mode compliance;
  • Redesign of the whole dark mode theme.

Making do with a limited palette

Mundipagg's branding from the parent company was minimal and the brand colors are turquoise, green, and blue. There was a need for shades and tints to be iterated from the brand colors to create buttons, badges, alerts, and accent text.

To meet the different design needs and visual hierarchy levels of headers, background and text, it became clear our current neutrals' palette was incomplete. Currently, the neutrals' palette encompasses 6 dark tones and 6 light tones, alternated in use (text and background) to create a light and a dark theme.

To easy semantically identify the 4 different stages of attention to users (success, error, warning, and information), Emerald has a semantic UI color palette adapted for dark and light mode, with shadow and accent colors (base color, shades, and tints).

Redesign proposal

To meet the expectations of users and staff increasingly more supportive of dark mode theme on interfaces, it was made an extensive chromatic study to reevaluate and redesign our current dark mode theme option.

  • Observation of chromatic hierarchies, not only invert colors values for dark and light mode;
  • Respect visual depth cues and the conventional use of shadows, even in dark mode;
  • Suggestion of a much more ample neutrals color palette to better provide division of space and hierarchic relationships;
  • Suggestion of two distinct but cohesive chromatic families for both dark and light mode.

The problem

Why a theme made to reduce eye strain was not bringing visual comfort?

A broken dark mode

As previously stated, our users and staff were opting for our dark mode - but it was not a good one. It was a proof of concept and mostly used as a visualization tool to sell the idea of a design system to the company.

Talking to developers, they agreed that our dark mode theme was not as "cool to look at" as others, but couldn't really articulate why. Still, even bad dark mode was better than no dark mode, and not only for our devs, but for users too, since it was being so widely preferred.

Our bad dark mode gave no visual comfort, which got me thinking: what's wrong with it? And how do we fix it?

References and studies

We learned a lot about visual depth cues. It was quite humbling to see that many principles that are no-brainers when dealing with a light interface became weird and foreign and harder to grasp when designing a dark interface.

The more I dug into how to better construct a full color palette to fulfill dark and light interface needs, the more I realized that we needed a lot more neutrals than we had.

Key takeaways

Too many neutrals is just enough neutrals

We needed a much more robust palette of neutrals than we first anticipated to convey the right depth cues intended for a dark mode theme effectively.

Remember the secondary brand colors

Turquoise is a complicated primary brand color to work with when there's not much branding elements or supporting graphic assets. The tertiary brand color, a beautiful dark blue, was actually a better alternative to exploit in light mode.

Follow the depth cues

Interfaces try to emulate real life, and the closer an object is, the brighter and lighter it appears. The further away it is, the darker and dimmer it appears.

Results

Our redesign over exceeded the product team's expectations. We used color to solve not only a seemingly aesthetic problem (improve the dark mode) but actually tackled accessibility and usability issues.

We received compliments from users and from staff, and it was very satisfying when developers of other squads would come to my desk to compliment me on the "new dark mode" in person.

Brand colors

We proposed no brand color changes, but we did implement the use of the tertiary brand color (dark blue) in buttons and accents in light mode instead of the primary brand color.

Furthermore, in dark mode, we kept the primary brand color and changed the use of a white text to the secondary green shade color.

We used the Sketch Color Contrast Analyzer Plugin to assure the AAA WCAG 2.1 accessibility standard on text + background.

Neutrals

It became necessary to establish a division between chromatic families intended for use in dark mode and light mode, to simplify the implementation process and avoid that each theme was just the chromatic inverse of the other.

Semantic UI palette

The semantic states color palette (success, error, alert, and information) was updated to pass effectively the accessibility test and also bring more cohesion to the overall chromatic unity of the whole palette.

The new darkmode

Now, with the new color palette, it was possible to build a much more visually pleasing, comfortable and semantically accurate dark mode.

A brand new color story

Observing updated studies and cases in dark mode and design of dark interfaces, we delivered the new color palette and the redesign of the Dark Mode theme on all Mundipagg's product dashboards.

We altered background color, a more vast choice of grays for hierarchy, relationships between background colors and titles in cards and boxes, badge colors, and button colors.

What about the light mode?

Although what motivated this redesign was the need for a better dark mode, the new neutral chromatic family enhanced the light mode experience as well. With a more robust palette, it was possible to design a more cohesive dashboard interface, exploring the grays for a more suitable and visually comfortable experience in light mode.
